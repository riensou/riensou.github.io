## 1.1 Chemistry in Context

Technological history is one of converting matter into different forms. Whether it be shaping a rock to make it sharper, using fire to cook and make pottery, or synthesizing drugs to treat disease, the principal goal is always control of matter.

> Chemistry is sometimes referred to as “the central science” due to its interconnectedness with a vast array of other STEM disciplines

- chemical physics, nuclear chemistry
- mathematics, computer science, information theory
- biochemistry, medicine, pharmacology, biotechnology, botany
- chemical engineering, materials science, nanotechnology
- agriculture, food science
- environmental science, geology, oceanography, atmospheric science
- astronomy, cosmology

<u>Chemistry</u> is the study of the composition, properties, and interactions of matter. The science of chemistry is done via the scientific method.

Chemistry covers three domains: (i) the macroscopic domain, (ii) the microscopic domain, and (iii) the symbolic domain. The macroscopic domain refers to large objects one can regularly sense. The microscopic domain refers to smaller objects one can either see with assist of a tool or cannot see but can experiment on. The symbolic domain is the language used to represent the prior domains.

## 1.2 Phases and Classifications of Matter

<u>Matter</u> is anything that occupies space and has mass. On Earth, there are three main states of matter:
- Solid (fixed shape and volume)
- Liquid (takes shape of container with fixed volume)
- Gas (expands to fill container)

In the interior of stars, another state of matter forms known as plasma.

Matter can be divided into two groups: <u>pure substances</u> and <u>mixtures</u>. Pure substances can be further broken down into either <u>compounds</u> or <u>elements</u>. Mixtures can be further broken down into either a <u>heterogenous mixture</u> or <u>homogenous mixture</u> (<u>solution</u>).

## 1.3 Physical and Chemical Properties

> A <u>physical property</u> is a characteristic of matter that is not associated with a change in its chemical composition... The ability to change from one type of matter into another (or the inability to change) is a <u>chemical property</u>.

If a property depends on the amount of matter present it is referred to as an <u>extensive property</u>. An example of this would be mass. If it does not depend on the amount of matter present it is referred to as an <u>intensive property</u>. An of example of this would be temperature.

## 1.4 Measurements

<u> Units</u> are standards of comparison for measurements. The following are the base units of the SI System:
- length - meter (m)
- mass - gram (g)
- time - second (s)
- temperature - kelvin (K)
- electric current - ampere (A)
- amount of substance - mole (mol)
- luminous intensity - candela (cd)

## 1.5 Measurement Uncertainty, Accuracy, and Precision

Measurements inherently introduce some level of uncertainty. Thus, numbers must be treated with some skepticism, thus we use <u>significant figures</u>.

<u>Precision</u> is when a result is highly repetitive and yields similar results. <u>Accuracy</u> is when a result is very close to the true or accepted value.
