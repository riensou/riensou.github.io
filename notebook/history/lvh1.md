# LVH I: Definitions and Preface

## Preface

Ex-president Ulysses Grant travels the world on a grand tour that is highly publicized and followed by many Americans. In it, he travels through Europe, Egypt, India, China, and Japan. In particular during his journies, he notes many places that have just undergone mass famine. His trip coincides with places that have undergone famine during the late nineenth century.

> It was almost as if the Americans were inadvertently following in the footprints of a monster whose colossal trail of destruction extended from the Nile to the Yellow Sea. 

Davis provides context that there were those at the time who believed that what happened was a political tragedy, not a natural disaster. This confronts an arugment that what occurred was not some sin of imperialism, but rather a tragedy of the natural world. Even before explaining the nuances of what occurred and how it worked, we are presented with the idea that this was a known phenomenon even at the time, that the evil of imperialism caused unparalleled levels of death and destruction. 

> Thus the Radical journalist William Digby, principal chronicler of the 1876 Madras famine, prophesized on the eve of Queen Victoria’s death that when “the part played by the British Empire in the nineteenth century is regarded by the historian fifty years hence, the unnecessary deaths of millions of Indians would be its principal and most notorious monument.”

Here, Davis makes an interesting observation relating the history and how we teach it. 

> But while the Dickensian slum remains in the world history curriculum, the famine children of 1876 and 1899 have disappeared. Almost without exception, modern historians writing about nineteenth-century world history from a metropolitan vantage-point have ignored the late Victorian megadroughts and famines that engulfed what we now call the “third world.”

> We not are dealing, in other words, with “lands of famine” becalmed in stagnant backwaters of world history, but with the fate of tropical humanity at the precise moment (1870–1914) when its labor and products were being dynamically conscripted into a London-centered world economy.

> Although equations may be more fashionable, it is necessary to pin names and faces to the human agents of such catastrophes, as well as to understand the configuration of social and natural conditions that constrained their decisions.

The beginning of this book spends its time elucidating the political and economic failures and how they lead to famine in what we now refer to as the third world. It will also discuss how local communites reacted to famine, noting the state terror that suppressed insurrections. Later in the book, the role of Nature is discussed; the phenomenon of El Niño and La Niña patterns are discussed. 

> The entire vast see-saw of air mass and ocean temperature, which extends into the Indian Ocean as well, is formally known as “El Niño-Southern Oscillation” (or ENSO, for short).

> As other historians have recently pointed out, when the Bastille was being stormed, the vertical class divisions inside the world’s major societies were not recapitulated as dramatic income difefrences between societies. The differences in living standards, say, between a French sans-culotte and Deccan farmer were relatively insignificant compared to the gulf that separated both from their ruling classes. By the end of Victoria’s reign, however, the inequality of nations was as profound as the inequality of classes. Humanity had been irrevocably divided. And the famed “prisoners of starvation,” whom “The Internationale” urges to arise, were as much modern inventions of the late Victorian world as electric lights, Maxim guns and “scientific” racism. 

## A Note on Definitions

- El Niño: the warm phase of ENSO, associated with drought in much of the monsoon tropics and northern China.
- Drought: the interplay between natural water availability and the agricultural usage patterns for water of a region. More important than the total volume of rainfall is the distribution of rainfall relative to regular cycles.
- Famine (causality): determined by social factors, and in modern society characterized not by an insufficient amount of food, but rather an insufficient and inequal access to food.
- Famine (mortality): defining a famine is (as is true of many things) a political question. Just defining it as mass starvation ignores the spectrum of instances where hunger, poverty, and instability are widespread but deaths are low. In fact, high degrees of hunger in a society can make it more susceptible to epidemics, and pinning blame on either famine or disease is a fuzzy line that oversimplifies rather than clarifies.
